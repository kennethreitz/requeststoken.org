Kenneth writes software for humans, mostly in Python. He created Requests (now with the Python Software Foundation), Responder (which runs this site), httpbin.org, Pipenv, Records and Tablib. He is a Fellow of the Python Software Foundation (PSF) and served one year on its board. Lately he builds music with code: PyTheory and Interpretations, an album written entirely in Python. He didn’t create this token; he is its largest holder.

See his other projects, all open source.

Written about Kenneth

“On Kenneth Reitz” by William John Bert (DIAGRAM 12.6, a literary journal): a literary review that reads the Requests 1.0 library as a work of literature.

“Kenneth Reitz Should Be a Millionaire,” or “Introducing the Dollar Store” by Jonathan Tushman (May 22, 2013): after a talk by Kenneth at a Python meetup, Tushman proposed a GitHub “Dollar Store” where developers could pay $1 to support open-source projects like Requests.

“Kenneth Reitz: Exploring the Material World in an Electronic Universe” on The Leica Camera Blog (Sep 5, 2013): an interview about his black-and-white street photography with the Leica M Monochrom and a 1954 M3.

Talks

“Python 2.7 & Python 3: A Sacred Love Story” on YouTube from Twitter University (uploaded Jul 1, 2014): Kenneth, then Python evangelist at Heroku, on the state of Python, the division in the community, and how to move forward.

“Python For Humans” on YouTube from Next Day Video (uploaded Dec 13, 2014): a recording of Kenneth’s talk; the video description links chipy.org.

Other interesting reading

Pieces on Python’s past and future, fitting for a project about legacy.

“Python Packaging: Hate, hate, hate everywhere” by Armin Ronacher (Jun 22, 2012): a look back at the state and history of Python’s packaging tools, more reflective than the title suggests.

“The Future of Python is in the Past” by Lyndsy Simon (Sep 24, 2012): a reply to Calvin Spealman’s worry about Python’s future, arguing that Python’s skilled community owes something to its barrier to entry, and cautioning against pushing it into the browser.

“Where does all the effort go? Looking at Python core developer activity” by Łukasz Langa (Oct 18, 2021): a data-driven look, drawn from CPython’s Git and GitHub history, at who maintains Python and where the work goes.

“The Inevitability of Mixing Open Source and Money” by Armin Ronacher (Oct 14, 2024): on why the current state of open-source funding is inadequate, especially for small projects.

Family history

Kenneth keeps a family index, a book of personal ancestry going back 17 generations. By his account, the surname Reitz comes chiefly from the Hessen-Nassau region of Germany and derives from the old word “ric,” meaning power. His paternal line runs from Georg Rudolph Reidt (around 1640) through the Reitz family that settled in Pennsylvania.

Open-source genome data

Kenneth has published his raw genotype data: a 23andMe export of about 960,000 SNPs (tab-separated text, reference build 37), under his own license: free to use “without restriction, except for uses related to cloning.” Make a clone and you owe “$20/day of life” (in 2011 dollars) until its “18th OOT (out of tube) anniversary,” then a 10% royalty on the clone’s income, paid quarterly.
